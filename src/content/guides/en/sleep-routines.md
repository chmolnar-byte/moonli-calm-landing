## The short answer

An evening with a baby often runs long because everyone is tired and nobody remembers what comes next. A sleep routine is not a timetable in that moment. It is a small, repeated order the baby can recognise: things are getting darker, quieter, closer to sleep. Three steps you can really keep are worth more than a twelve-point ritual that only works on the good days.

The NHS describes simple helps: a calm, dim room, a familiar order, patience. None of that promises a baby will sleep through the night. It makes the crossing less abrupt. That alone can make an evening kinder.

## What a routine can do

It tells the body that the day is getting an edge. Lights down, voice down, the same sequence. For a small baby that is an anchor in a day that otherwise feels like milk, a diaper, and being awake, in no particular order.

It cannot undo overtiredness. If the wake window closed a long time ago, the loveliest song still becomes an argument. So the look at the day belongs before the ritual. If you only notice at seven that the last nap was at half past two, the evening has already started in arrears. That is not a failure. That is a day when the information arrived late.

A routine is also allowed to stay small when you are small. After a short night, sometimes all that fits is: dim the room, breast or bottle, lie down. The song can come back tomorrow. Consistency means the signals return. It does not mean you put on a performance every evening.

## Sleep routine by age

### 0 to 3 months

Nights are still in pieces, and a fixed sequence at seven o'clock is often more than you can promise. What carries you are quiet repetitions: the same light, the same voice, the same order around the night feed. The sleep place stays safe beside that. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends, in the first year, back sleeping, a firm surface, and no loose fabric in the sleep space. A ritual does not change that foundation. It only sits gently in front of it.

### 4 to 6 months

Many families notice now, for the first time, that a short evening edge helps. A wash, a darker room, a song, lying down. Four steps. If lying down still escalates, look at the wake window first. Often the day was awake too long, and the ritual gets blamed for a tiredness that was already overdue.

### 7 to 12 months

Teeth, solids, new movement: the evening gets restless even though you already know the sequence. That stings, because it feels as if the whole thing has vanished. Usually it is a phase, not proof that the routine "did not work". In those weeks, shorten it. Fewer steps, the same order, more patience with yourselves.

### From 12 months

The sequence can grow a little: one more story, a short tidy-up together. Weeknights and weekends can sit near each other on the clock. They do not have to be sacred. A late afternoon with visitors knocks one evening over. The next day you return to the familiar edge, without putting yesterday's evening on trial again.

## A sequence in five steps

1. Look at the day before you start. When was the last sleep? Does the baby already look past tired?
2. Bring light and noise down. The room is allowed to become boring.
3. A familiar order, three to five steps at most. The same ones.
4. Lie them down while they can still follow. Awake enough to know the place, tired enough to let go.
5. If it goes wrong: make it shorter. A second programme on the same evening rarely helps.

The fifth point is the one that is hardest to believe. Once the crying has started, you want to do more. Another song, more walking, the light on and off again. Often the evening needs the opposite: a smaller circle, and an earlier start tomorrow.

## How Moonli keeps the evening light

### See the day before the ritual starts

Sleep, feeds and diapers live in one place. You do not have to rebuild the afternoon from memory while the baby is already fussing. The wake-window guide tells you whether you are inside the range or already past it. Tracking stays free.

### The phase, instead of a search at 3 a.m.

In the info hub you can see, roughly, which phase the baby is in. That takes away the search at three in the morning, when you want to know whether teeth, a leap, or simply a normal restless night might be behind it. It is a way of placing the night. It is not a diagnosis.

### The last signal, without a phone in the child's hands

A short story or a steady sound can be the last step. You hold the phone. The child does not. When nothing else works, that is a reach you can use. It is not a promise that quiet will follow.

## What you can leave out this week

Leave the perfect sequence. Pick three steps that still work on a bad day, and repeat them for a week. Log the sleep, so that in the evening you can see whether the window had already closed.

If one night goes completely sideways, the next evening is still an evening with the same three steps. A routine grows on the ordinary days. The extraordinary ones, you are allowed to simply get through.
