## The short answer

A sleep regression feels as if the progress of the last few weeks has vanished. Your baby wakes more often, naps for shorter stretches or fights being put down – even though the evening routine was working yesterday. The word sounds like a step backwards. For many families it is closer to this: the body is learning something new, and sleep needs a few nights to catch up.

You do not have to invent a new programme in this phase. What holds is often the same as before: a safe place to sleep, a small evening ritual, and a look at the wake window before you stretch the evening. The American Academy of Pediatrics keeps to back sleeping, a firm surface and no loose bedding. A restless phase does not change that foundation.

## What a sleep regression is – and what it is not

Parents usually mean this: sleep had already been getting better, and now it tips over again. More frequent night waking, shorter daytime naps, fussing when you lay your baby down. Sometimes the baby is also practising new movements – reaching, rolling, sitting, crawling – and keeps practising at night.

Not every bad week is a sleep regression. An infection, a growth spurt without a “month label”, teething or a day with too many visitors can have the same effect. The label only helps if it reassures you: this phase often comes to an end. It does not help if you take every restless night as immediate proof that your routine has failed.

Scientifically, the change around the fourth month is the best described: sleep becomes more “grown-up”, with lighter and deeper parts. Many later waves are less clearly defined. For everyday life, one distinction is enough: is the baby ill or hungry? Or does the day mainly look like too long awake and too little rest?

## Typical months at a glance

### Around 4 months

Often the first big wave. You can read more in the [4-month sleep regression](/en/guides/4-month-sleep-regression/) guide. In short: the sleep structure changes, and many babies need clearer transitions and shorter wake windows than the week before.

### Around 8 to 10 months

Separation, movement, sometimes solids and teething all at once. Evenings that were going well get loud again. Shorten the routine rather than lengthening it. Three familiar steps carry more than a new programme.

### Around 12 months

Learning to walk, first words, one nap fewer – the day gets fuller. At night you often notice it as more frequent waking. The [evening routine](/en/guides/baby-sleep-routine/) can stay small. A [look at the wake window](/en/guides/baby-wake-windows/) before the ritual keeps overtiredness from being mistaken for a “regression”.

### Around 18 months

Will, closeness, dreams. Some children test boundaries, others simply need more reassurance. A fixed, short ritual in the same order helps more than debates at midnight.

Not every baby hits every phase. Some families only notice the four-month one, others mostly the eight-month one. If the chart does not fit, that is not a mistake – it is your child.

## What helps in these weeks

1. Look at the day before you try to rescue the evening. When was the last sleep? Does your baby already seem overtired?
2. Keep the routine small. The same three steps. Not longer, just more familiar.
3. Keep the safe sleep space. Back sleeping, a firm surface, no loose bedding in the cot.
4. Plan enough rest windows during the day. Many “regression nights” begin with an afternoon that ran too long.
5. One night is allowed to go wrong. The next evening you return to the edge of your routine without renegotiating the night before.

The NHS stresses patience and a calm environment. That sounds like very little when someone is screaming at three in the morning. It is still often the frame in which a phase fades again: not more programme, but less chaos around the signals.

## How Moonli keeps the overview

### Track sleep without lengthening the night

When you can see when the last sleep was, you decide sooner whether the window has already closed. Tracking stays free. You do not have to reconstruct the restless week from memory.

### Place the phase, without a diagnosis

In the info hub you can see roughly which developmental phase your baby is in. That takes away the search at three in the morning, when you want to know whether teething, a leap or simply an ordinary restless night could be behind it. It does not replace a medical assessment.

### One calm signal at the end

White noise or a short story can be the last signal. You hold the phone, your child does not. When nothing else works, that is one thing to reach for – not a promise that quiet follows.

## What you can leave out this week

Skip the new sleep training in the first restless night. Skip the ten-point checklist. Keep three signals, the safe sleep space and a look at the day. Write the sleep down so that in the evening you can see whether overtiredness was already there before the ritual.

If the nights mainly tip around the fourth month, read the [4-month sleep regression](/en/guides/4-month-sleep-regression/) guide for more depth. For the evening sequence, the [baby sleep routine](/en/guides/baby-sleep-routine/) remains the anchor. And if you are awake and exhausted at night: [awake at night and exhausted](/en/guides/awake-at-night-exhausted/) – without having to apologise for it.
