## The short answer

The first spoon is often more excitement than food. A little puree on the chin, a surprised face, and you are already wondering whether that was "right". At the beginning, solids are an addition to milk, not a meal plan you have to complete. What is worth writing down is small: what was new, roughly how much arrived, and whether anything stood out afterwards. The rest is allowed to stay as puree on the bib.

The WHO and the NHS talk about starting around six months, alongside continued breastfeeding or bottles. Whether your baby is ready is a conversation with a doctor or midwife. An app does not set that date.

## When families start at all

Many families notice the signs side by side: the baby can sit with support, the tongue thrust that pushes everything back out is fading, they reach for your food and bring things to their mouth. None of these signs alone is a starting gun. Together they are worth a conversation with someone who knows infants, not a weekly plan from the internet.

Milk stays the main meal through this time. The spoon is curiosity, practice, taste. If almost nothing stays in the mouth on a given day, that is an ordinary beginning. You have not failed because the plate is still full.

For some families the start is a joy. For some it is pressure, because suddenly everyone has an opinion: too early, too late, not enough vegetables, too much puree. You are allowed to narrow that down to one question. What was new today, and was the baby all right afterwards?

## What a useful note contains

- the food, concrete enough that you still recognise it a week later
- roughly how much arrived, in one sentence, not in grams you do not want to weigh
- the time, so you can see the gap from the milk feed
- whether anything stood out afterwards: skin, vomiting, a cry that felt different

Three new foods in one day make the note unreadable if something shows up. One new food, a few days of watching, then the next: that is slower, and kinder to both of you. You do not have to work through a whole weekly menu that somewhere counts as "ideal".

### What does not belong in the note

No score for how "well" they ate. No comparison with the child next door. No list of foods you mean to "get through" before the month ends. A baby's mouth learns at its own pace. A day with two spoons and a very clean bib is a complete day.

## When to stop and ask

Trouble breathing, swelling in the face, repeated vomiting, or a rash that worries you is not an entry for later. That is the moment for medical help, now. The note is useful afterwards, because you can say what it was and when. Before that, the note does not matter.

A single gag on a new piece, a face that clearly hates the taste, puree that comes straight back out: that is often practice, not an alarm. If you are unsure, unsure is a good reason to ask. You do not have to sort this out alone, and you do not have to talk it into being fine.

### Next to milk, sleep and growth

Solids sit beside milk feeds, sleep and the growth curve. You can see whether appetite has shifted without opening three places. If weight is on your mind, the curve is nearby, not in a separate table you have to maintain on the side.

### For both people who feed

When the other parent, a grandparent or childcare offers the next spoon, they can see what has already been tried. Nobody has to message to ask whether the carrot is already "done". The record is there. That removes one of those small mental-load questions that add up over weeks.

## A calm first week

Pick one food you and your doctor or midwife consider suitable. Offer it without having to empty the plate. Write the name, one sentence about the amount, and whether anything followed.

At the end of the week you have a short list instead of a bad conscience. Milk stays the foundation. The spoon is the start of something that takes months. You do not have to be good at it in seven days.
